## Page 1: Unit Introduction

Welcome to this unit. In this unit we will explore how Japanese people communicate, why misunderstandings happen even with the best intentions, and how to read what's really being said beneath the surface.

:::video{url="https://youtube.com/embed/PLACEHOLDER" title="Cultural Sensitivity Introduction" duration=180}
:::

**What You'll Learn:**

1. **How "Yes" works in Japan:** You will understand why a Japanese student might say "yes" to your invitation and never show up — and why that's not dishonesty, but kindness.

2. **How silence speaks louder than words:** You will learn why Japanese students go quiet in group discussions and how to create space where they feel safe to share.

3. **How group identity shapes everything:** You will discover why a student who was open with you one-on-one may ignore you when their friends are around — and why that's actually an opportunity, not a rejection.

## Page 2: When "Yes" Doesn't Mean "Yes"

:::video{url="https://youtube.com/embed/PLACEHOLDER" title="Understanding Honne and Tatemae" duration=240}
:::

**The Inquiry:** Why would someone agree to come to your event and then not show up?

:::activity{title="The Two-Sided Coin"}
**Setup:** Present this scenario: "You invite Kenji to a special gathering on Saturday. He smiles and says 'Yes, I would like to come!' Saturday arrives. Kenji is not there. You feel confused, maybe even lied to."

**Investigation:** Introduce the concepts of Honne and Tatemae:
- **Honne** — a person's true feelings or desires
- **Tatemae** — the polite, socially appropriate response

Consider these questions:
- In your culture, what does 'yes' mean? In Japanese culture, 'yes' might mean 'I'm listening,' 'I understand,' or 'I hear you, but I may not agree.' Why would a culture develop this distinction?
- If saying 'no' directly could damage a relationship, and relationships are the highest social value, what would you do?

**Discovery:** Kenji wasn't lying. He was protecting the relationship. In Japanese culture, a direct "no" can feel aggressive and disrespectful. Saying "yes" is an act of relational care, not deception.

**Key Takeaway:** Pay attention to nonverbal cues and context. Offer easy "outs" like: "Only if you're free — no pressure at all!" Build trust so they feel safe being honest with you.
:::

:::reflection{id="r1"}
Think about a time someone told you what you wanted to hear instead of the truth. How did it feel when you found out? Now flip it — can you think of a time YOU avoided saying 'no' to protect someone's feelings? What does this tell you about the gap between your culture and Japan's?
:::

## Page 3: The Power of Silence

**The Inquiry:** You're leading a small group discussion. You ask a great question. Your American teammates jump in with answers. The Japanese students say nothing. What went wrong?

:::activity{title="The 30-Second Experiment"}
**Setup:** After asking a question, wait 30 full seconds before speaking again. Most Americans break silence after 3-5 seconds. Japanese conversation rhythm allows for 15-30 seconds of thoughtful pause.

**Investigation:** Japanese students may stay silent for four key reasons:
- **Fear of imperfect English** — even fluent students hesitate if they can't express themselves perfectly
- **Not interrupting** — in Japan, interrupting is impolite. People wait for a clear, complete pause
- **Forming a complete answer** — Japanese prefer thoughtful, well-formed responses over quick reactions
- **High-context communication** — Japan has a saying: "Hear one, understand ten." When someone says 70% of their meaning, they expect the listener to infer the other 30%

Consider:
- Which of these four reasons would be hardest for YOU to navigate? Why?
- How does your typical conversation style (fast-paced, jumping in, finishing sentences) look from a Japanese perspective?

**Discovery:** Silence is not disengagement. It is respect and thoughtfulness. When you rush to fill silence, you may accidentally communicate that their thoughts aren't worth waiting for.

**Key Takeaway:** Slow down. Call on students gently and individually. Send discussion topics ahead of time if possible. Give space — and watch what happens when you do.
:::

:::reflection{id="r2"}
On a scale of 1-10, how comfortable are you with silence in a conversation? What does silence 'mean' in your family or friend group? How might you need to retrain your instincts for Japan?
:::

## Page 5: Etiquette Essentials

**The Inquiry:** What are the small behaviors that Japanese people notice immediately — even if they're too polite to tell you?

### Quick Reference Guide

**1. Names and Honorifics**
- Add "-san" after someone's name (Riku-san, Tanaka-san). It's the equivalent of Mr./Mrs. but works for everyone.
- Use family names unless invited to use first names. First names are reserved for close relationships.
- When in doubt, listen to how Japanese people address each other and mirror it.

**2. Bowing**
- A slight bow (15°) is a casual greeting. A deeper bow shows more respect.
- You don't need to master this — a genuine attempt is appreciated. Japanese people have lower expectations of foreigners than of each other.

**3. Public Behavior**
- Keep volume low on trains and in neighborhoods (especially mornings and evenings in Kyoto)
- Stand in single-file lines; wait for others to exit before boarding
- Don't talk on the phone on public transportation
- Don't block aisles or doorways when in groups

**4. Chopsticks**
- Never stick chopsticks upright in rice (it resembles a funeral ritual)
- Don't pass food chopstick-to-chopstick (same reason)

**5. Physical Contact**
- Japan is a non-touch culture. Beyond handshakes, physical contact can give the wrong impression.
- Be aware of how affection between team members looks to Japanese observers — they may assume you're dating.

:::info-box{heading="Cultural Awareness Tip"}
These etiquette points aren't about perfection — they're about showing respect and removing unnecessary barriers to the gospel. Japanese people are gracious with foreigners who make honest mistakes, but they notice effort and care.
:::

## Page 6: Lesson Summary

This lesson reveals that cultural sensitivity in Japan is not about memorizing rules — it's about understanding the values beneath the behavior.

:::key-point{heading="Communication"}
**"Yes" protects relationships, silence shows respect, and 70% of meaning lives beneath the surface.** Your job is to listen for what's NOT being said.
:::

:::key-point{heading="Identity"}
**Japanese students live within a web of group belonging.** A student may be spiritually curious alone but cautious in front of friends. Minister through the group, not around it.
:::

:::key-point{heading="Presence"}
**Small behaviors speak louder than your words.** Your volume, your clothing, how you use chopsticks, whether you touch someone's arm — these things matter. Remove every barrier except the Cross itself.
:::
